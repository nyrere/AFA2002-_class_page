Teaching Philosophy

Teaching Statement

I believe education should push students to engage with material outside their own norms, helping them become more understanding and conscious members of society. Everything I plan to do in the classroom — from how I design assignments to how I approach new technology like AI — is built around that goal.

Students' Learning

Students come to learning with different motivations. Some are goal-oriented, working toward a finished product or outcome. Others are learning-oriented, motivated simply by the desire to know more. I design my courses so that both kinds of learners find something to hold onto — but I lean especially into goal orientation, because students engage deeply when they care about the subject or have a clear objective they're working toward. My role is to help students see the connection between the material in front of them and a larger goal beyond the classroom.

Providing students with diverse points of view and multiple ways of considering a problem is paramount to the humanities as a discipline. In my "Viewing the Black Woman" course, several weeks are structured around sustained discussion as part of the course progression. I emphasize openness to opposing thought — I'm cognizant that this subject matter is polarizing by its very nature, so I intentionally make space for active discussion, not only between myself and the class as a whole, but student-to-student, so that everyone can bring their own worldview into contact with the material. Over the course of a semester, I work to build an environment where students become comfortable sharing their own views and, just as importantly, open to sitting with views that differ from their own.

A Safe, Respectful Space for Discussion

I value inclusivity and respect equally in my teaching environment. I learned from my years as a university advisor that setting a precedent early in a student interaction shapes the tone of everything that follows. I carry that lesson into the classroom: I make it clear to students that they are never obligated to disclose personal information to me or others, and that they have every right to think through their own ideas privately. If a student chooses to ask me for personal advice, I give it — but only then. Three things are central to maintaining respect in this type of exchange: acknowledging consent, not offering unsolicited opinions, and giving people the time they need to arrive at their own conclusions.

My Evolving Approach to AI

My stance on classroom Artificial Intelligence use has changed substantially, and that evolution is worth naming. I previously held a strict no-use policy, largely because I hadn't taken the time to understand what these tools do. That changed during my graduate course, ENG 6813 (Interdisciplinary Teaching), where I was confronted with the reality of AI's capabilities rather than relying on assumptions born from a lack of knowledge in the space. My ideology going forward is grounded in experimentation, informed by work like Ethan Mollick's One Useful Thing, which speaks to the practical, everyday uses of AI in the classroom.

My AI policy reflects that shift: students can use AI to generate examples, summarize notes, and create study aids, but it never serves as the sole source for a research assignment. Students remain responsible for everything they submit under their own name. I want students to internalize that AI can be a useful tool, but they are the researchers — and eventually the experts — of their own work. That sense of accountability is something I hope follows them well beyond my classroom.

Digital Humanities in My Course Design

One area of Digital Humanities methodology I lean into heavily is examining how digital platforms interact with humanist inquiry, particularly through the lens of Black digital humanities. This connection is central to my teaching philosophy and shapes how I design assignments from the very start of the course.

Early in my course design for AFA 2002 (Viewing the Black Woman), students complete an assignment built specifically around AI, because I want to make clear from the outset that a system of racism — both mediated and unmediated — has long structured the exclusion of the Black diaspora, and that this history persists in the tools we now use. Students evaluate common tropes of Black women in television by interacting directly with an AI platform, then comparing its output to their own independent interpretation of the same material, allowing them to witness, in real time, the bias and misinformation that can exist in AI databases. This assignment facilitates learning of Black digital humanities ideologies: helping students use technology effectively while remaining sharply aware of its shortcomings, and recognizing exactly where their own original thought is not just useful, but necessary.

Assessment as Feedback

As a student, I value receiving quality and specific feedback on my work, and I carry that motivation directly into my teaching. I aim to give students concrete points they can act on to improve, and I acknowledge their growth when they do. Feedback, to me, is not a judgment on a finished product — it's part of an ongoing conversation about how a student is developing.

Course-Wide AI Policy

Using AI Responsibly

How AI chatbots may — and may not — be used across AFA 2004: Viewing the Black Woman.

Pretending AI tools don't exist doesn't prepare you to think critically about when they should or shouldn't be used. In this course, you're allowed to use AI chatbots — specifically Anthropic's Claude, Microsoft Copilot, and ChatGPT. No single tool is required for any assignment. If you use AI on an assignment, you need to disclose it, including a brief note on how you used it. Some assignments narrow or expand this general policy in their own instructions — always check the specific assignment sheet.

Think of AI as a research assistant, not a ghostwriter. A good research assistant can dig up sources, summarize background, or point you toward context you might've missed — but they don't write your paper for you, and they don't get to decide what you conclude. If AI ends up doing the thinking instead of you, that work isn't really yours anymore.

AI tools pull from real people's work, so we honor that labor by citing sources properly using APA (7th edition) format. You're responsible for any false or misrepresented information in your work, even if AI produced it.

Course Description

About This Course

In this course, you'll explore how Black women — especially African American women — are portrayed in the media all around us: music, poetry, literature, film, social media, and visual art like painting, photography, and sculpture. You'll pay close attention to work made by Black creators about Black women, looking at the stories, images, and songs that shape how Black womanhood gets seen. No prior background in African American or media studies is required. By the end, you'll be equipped to critically consider and draw evidence-based conclusions about racial- and gender-based stereotypes, agency, and representation in media.

The Core Question

“How is the Black woman being represented?”

That question anchors every unit of the course — the throughline for every case study, discussion, and assignment from the first week to the final presentation.

Learning Outcomes

What Students Can Do by the End

Five outcomes guide the course design, from the first discussion post to the final presentation.

  • 01Build a clear, research-backed argument about how a Black woman is represented in a media work, using credible sources to support the point.
  • 02Identify and critically evaluate major stereotypes and tropes associated with the representation of Black women in media — Mammy, Jezebel, the "angry Black woman," colorism/light-skin bias.
  • 03Build cultural awareness and compassion for engaging with representation and bias in news, advertising, and entertainment media.
  • 04Conduct independent research using scholarly, historical, and creator-background sources, citing properly in APA (7th edition) format.
  • 05Evaluate the strengths and limitations of AI tools when used for research and analysis of race- and gender-based representation.

Signature Assignment · 100 Points

Being Seen — The Presentation

The capstone assignment of the course, where students bring every analytical tool from the semester to a piece of media of their own choosing.

By the end of the semester, students will be able to construct and defend a research-supported argument about how Black women are portrayed in media — painting, text, film, photograph, song, and more — citing credible sources to support their position. Students will build the cultural awareness and compassion needed to critically engage with representation and bias in news, advertising, and entertainment media. — Course Goal, Viewing the Black Woman
✦ ✦ ✦

Assignment Goals

Students select a piece of media — painting, text, film, photograph, song, or other artistic work — and conduct independent research to answer: "How are Black women being represented in this work?"

The final presentation must (a) state a clear thesis addressing this question, (b) synthesize evidence from at least 3 sources (scholarly criticism, historical context, or creator background), and (c) properly cite all sources. Students also reflect on why they chose the piece, what they learned, how their research shaped their perspective, and what challenges they encountered.

Four Tasks

How the Assignment Unfolds

1

Choose One Form of Media to Analyze

  • At least one Black woman must be showcased in the piece. Cannot use a piece already discussed in the course.
  • Examples could include a music video, song lyrics, a movie, a television show, a book, a poem, a meme, a performance, physical art (painting, drawing, sculpture), a court case, or a viral TikTok.
  • If choosing a movie, show, or book, focus on one of the following: a specific scene, plot point, or character.
  • Feel free to extend beyond these examples.
2

Examine the Black Woman in the Context of the Piece

  • Include the creator/author/originator, identify subject matter, and include background — setting, cultural relevance, historical context, tropes, etc.
  • Look at how the Black woman's presence affects — or is affected by — the work.
  • Keep a record of the sources of your research; this should be included in the reflection and mentioned in the presentation where applicable.
3

Create a Presentation About Your Medium 70 pts

  • Between 4 and 12 minutes.
  • Include a thesis statement in the first minute of the presentation addressing "How are Black women being represented?"
  • Consider items like narrative point of view, casting, symbolism, dialogue, word choice, and author/producer motivations.
  • Include a moving element (PowerPoint, slide deck, video, song, voice recording, graphic, etc.).
  • Provide a link to the piece, or include a picture or link to a summary of the piece, for a book, television show, or movie.
4

Create a Reflection 30 pts

  • Include a separate video or written piece explaining why you chose the piece you did and why you chose to present the information in the manner you did.
  • Include citations for research.
  • No word count or time length required; both questions must be addressed for full credit.

AI Integration

Using AI on This Assignment

Any AI software used must be disclosed in the reflection's citations, along with its purpose.

✓ Acceptable Uses

  • Brainstorming
  • Clarifying concepts, terms, or historical/cultural context encountered during research
  • Checking grammar, spelling, and citation formatting in the final presentation and reflection
  • Organizing the layout of the presentation

✕ Non-Acceptable Uses

  • Choosing the presentation piece — students should pick something already relevant or interesting to them
  • Substituting for reading or viewing the source material
  • Writing the reflection on the student's behalf

Students remain responsible for any misrepresented, misquoted, or false information AI produces. If a grammar/spelling checker is used on the reflection, both the original and rewritten text must be included.

Worked Example · Being Seen

The Jezebel Trope & Pearline in Sinners

A model submission built to show students exactly what a strong presentation and reflection look like.

Thesis

Pearline's story arc in Sinners is a layered approach that, at times, is a contradictory answer to the question of how Black women are represented in this film: she is granted moments of desire, agency, and heroism, yet she is also defined largely by her relationship to men, denied the same narrative recognition as her male counterpart, and ultimately sacrificed for someone else's story.

Media
Feature Film
Released
April 18, 2025
Writer & Director
Ryan Coogler
Character of Interest
Pearline, played by Jayme Lawson
Setting
Jim Crow-era, 1930s Mississippi Delta

Why the Director's Background Matters

Ryan Coogler, an African American filmmaker from the San Francisco Bay Area, has a track record of complex Black storytelling — Fruitvale Station (2013), Creed (2015), and Black Panther (2018). That background is relevant to Pearline being a more well-rounded character, even as she still operates within a traditional trope.

The Jezebel was a middle-aged or young woman governed by her libido and "matters of the flesh." She was deemed a hypersexual woman with unlimited bestial passions. — Mgadmi, "Black Women's Identity: Stereotypes, Respectability and Passionlessness (1890–1930)," para. 5

Historically, white women were portrayed as models of self-respect and modesty, while Black women were cast as innately promiscuous — a depiction signified by the name Jezebel (Pilgrim). The trope is usually marked by suggestive dress, an outspoken or brash personality, and definition through connections to men.

The Analysis

Reading Pearline Against the Trope

First Impressions and Setting

When we first meet Pearline, she is unattended at the train station, and the film gives no indication of where she's going or has been. The first thing established about her character is that she is married — yet she's shown flirting with Sammie, positioning her early on as a love interest rather than a character with her own independent arc. She's associated first with her relationship to a man the audience never meets, and being unaccompanied at a train station signals a kind of worldliness. It's implied she's older than Sammie, which both are aware of — adding to a persuasive, predatory characterization that aligns her again with the Jezebel trope.

Juxtaposition with Annie

Pearline is set against Annie, the film's other central Black woman character. Pearline is portrayed as skinny and conventionally attractive by white beauty standards, while Annie is cast in the "mammy" archetype, loyal to a man (Smoke) who ultimately left her — a contrast that raises questions about which body types are afforded desirability. Though Pearline is an adulteress, the film doesn't dwell on or condemn this, since every character is presented as a sinner in their own right. She's also given an experience of oral sex that appears to be a first for her — a moment suggesting a Black woman can be portrayed as desirable in her own right rather than solely as an object for male satisfaction — even as her reputation as a late-night-club singer suggests she was already understood as somewhat promiscuous before Sammie enters her story.

Characterization Continued

Pearline tells Sammie she walked to the Juke Joint alone at night — a choice that would have been dangerous for any woman in the 1930s, and now — suggesting she's done so before and adding to a read of promiscuity or "wildness." She also dances openly with Sammie, suggesting she's unafraid of being caught by her husband, or simply doesn't care. The film doesn't explore this further, because doing so isn't necessary to Sammie's story.

Erasure and Lack of Recognition

Before taking the stage, Sammie receives a formal introduction — Pearline performs without one. That absence suggests her background and identity are treated as irrelevant to the story, even as she plays a significant role within it.

Selflessness and Sacrifice

Pearline ultimately gives up her life to help Sammie escape the vampires, positioning the Black woman as selfless in service of protecting others in her community — a final beat that closes out the film's contradictory portrait of her.
